    Compare profiles

    Six shapes, drawn to true scale. Area decides how hard a vane steers; height decides whether it clears your rest.

    Measured from each profile's printed curve at its standard length. Use them as a starting point; your shaft, point weight and twist all change how an arrow flies.

    Steering, noise and speed

    Height is what pulls a broadhead back on line, which is why hunting vanes stand taller than target vanes. Taller vanes are also louder and need more rest clearance.

    Length adds drag, but spreading the same area over a longer, lower vane is quieter than a short, steep one. A long vane can be quiet; a tall one rarely is.

    Shaft size matters too. Thin shafts need less vane to spin and stabilize; fatter shafts need more.

    Spin: straight, offset and helical

    A spinning arrow evens out small flaws in flight instead of letting them steer it off line. Twist is what makes it spin, and more twist means more spin and a little more drag.

    Our degrees are the vane's angle to the shaft, seen from above, the way most fletching jigs are labeled. Some jigs quote degrees per inch of vane, which reads higher for the same twist. The builder also shows how far each vane wraps around your shaft, which depends on your vane length and shaft size.

    Long vanes catch more air at the same angle, so we cap helical by length: up to 4° on vanes 2.25in and shorter, 3° up to 2.70in, and 2° above that. Offset comes in 1° and 2°. Past about 3°, more twist mostly adds drag and drop at distance.

    Right or left?

    Right spins the arrow clockwise as it flies away from you; left spins it counter-clockwise. Neither is more accurate. We default to right because screw-in points and inserts are right-hand threaded, so a clockwise spin tends to snug them rather than loosen them. Pick left only if you shoot single-bevel heads made for a left spin, and keep every arrow in your quiver the same.

    3 vanes or 4?

    Three vanes is the standard: less drag, a flatter path and less wind drift. Four adds about a third more surface, for quicker recovery and more say over a big broadhead, at some cost in speed. If you go to four, consider a smaller profile so the total area stays about the same.

    Slide on. Peel off.

    About a minute per arrow. All you need is glue and, for removal, a utility knife.

    Putting a vane on

    1. Add two rings of glue

      A small ring near the front of the vane's opening and another in the middle.

    2. Slide it down the shaft

      Push it on from the nock end until it sits where you want it.

    3. Hold it a few seconds

      Keep it still while the glue grabs. No clamps and no waiting on the next vane.

    A little glue goes a long way. Too much just squeezes out the ends.

    Replacing a damaged vane

    1. Cut along the spine

      Run a utility knife along the spine of the sleeve. Keep the blade shallow: you're cutting the vane, not the shaft.

    2. Peel it off

      The sleeve comes away in one piece. The other arrows don't need touching.

    3. Slide on a new one

      Same as putting one on. Done.

    Made by a shooter, not a factory

    Why this exists

    This started with two annoyances, not a business plan: showing up to hunt or shoot with a torn vane and no fletching jig to fix it, and scratching arrow shafts nearly every time I stripped off old vanes.

    So the requirement was simple. It had to go on and come off without a jig, without scratching anything, and fast enough to do standing in a field. Glue, a knife, about thirty seconds: slide a new vane down the shaft, and if one gets torn up mid-hunt, cut it off, peel it away and slide the next one on.

    I'm Isaac, and I design and print every one of these myself. It turned into a business when I realized other people were dealing with the same problem, and once the design worked there wasn't a good reason to keep it to myself.
